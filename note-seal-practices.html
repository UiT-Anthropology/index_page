Field story · Finland

Knowledge carried through materials

Sea-ice hunting, skins and shared practical work offer ways to understand the cultural relationships surrounding Baltic seals.

Spring 2026Adapted from Florian Stammler’s Finland fieldwork update

Learning on the ice

The fieldwork report describes a long trip around the turn of April and May, during which the researchers accompanied local hunters on the sea ice. They observed the patience, skill and practical judgement involved in finding and approaching a seal.

An important principle described by hosts was the ability to retrieve an animal after a shot. The choice of position therefore involved both marksmanship and a responsibility to recover the seal.

Making, using and passing on knowledge

The work continued through processing meat, handling skins and separating fat. Heidi Konttinen’s doctoral research focuses particularly on practices involving skins and fat.

The report also records local accounts of seal fat used in traditional paint. Such material practices connect hunting knowledge to the care of buildings, everyday skills and the transmission of cultural knowledge.

Relationships beyond an economic measure

The researchers describe sealing as emotionally and culturally significant to their hosts. Through participation, the team asks how management and public debate can recognise these relationships, alongside ecological concerns.

An ice-hunting material culture

The account identifies the Baltic ringed seal as the focus of the traditional hunt and describes ajopuu, a specially designed monoski used on the ice. The team’s trip around 30 April and 1 May lasted about 14 hours. Hunters’ knowledge concerned finding seals as well as approaching a position from which a hunted animal could be recovered.

Seasons, identity and recognition

Hosts describe a hunting season that historically began earlier, and the difficulty of fitting a later ice hunt around preparations for fishing as ice conditions change. The report emphasises the cultural and emotional significance of the practice alongside its costs.

Florian reports that Heidi Konttinen and the seal hunters association attempted to have the sealing culture included in Finland’s intangible cultural heritage list, and that the application was refused. This is presented as the fieldwork team’s account of its experience.

Materials across borders

The fieldwork account also compares local sealing practices with the circulation of skins from Greenland and describes seal fat used in traditional house paint. The complete source account preserves the author’s discussion of trade, regulation and material uses.

Source material: Finland fieldwork update · September 2026.
